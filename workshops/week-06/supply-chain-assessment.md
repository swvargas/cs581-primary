## Evidence and confidence conventions

**High:** bounded observation or high confidence that a cited source explicitly states a fact. **Medium:** supported engineering inference whose relevant prerequisites are partly established. **Low:** hypothetical applicability with important unverified prerequisites. **Unknown:** presence, exposure, exploitation or effectiveness not assessed sufficiently to decide. Source confidence is separate from PSI applicability and attack likelihood: a reliable advisory does not make an unverified product present. Host evidence and hashes are in [startup-gap-log.md](startup-gap-log.md); web queries/retrieval are in [session-log.md](session-log.md). All controls below are proposals, not completed remediation.

These are prioritized assessment candidates, not measured likelihood rankings. Vector 1 concerns observed software components, but their delivery/build path is unverified. Vector 2 concerns the scenario's external support relationship; a corresponding current lab supplier path is unverified. Vector 3 concerns possible physical-access equipment represented by the simulation; actual supplier/device presence is unknown. Ordinary local misuse without a supplier/delivery relationship is not, by itself, a supply-chain attack. A vulnerable legitimate supplied product is a supplier-component risk, but does not establish malicious compromise of its supplier or build pipeline.

**Verified baseline versus assurance:** active services, responding read interfaces and some SIEM receipts were observed. Earlier source inspection and package metadata are observations of files/environment, not attestation of code loaded by running services. No malicious package, compromised supplier, affected Armatura product, physical incident, or successful remediation was established. All physical impacts below are conditional potential consequences, not observed outcomes.

## Top three supply chain attack vectors

### Vector 1 — Substituted Python package or deployment artifact

**Chain:** attacker compromises a package publisher/build pipeline, dependency resolution or delivered application artifact → installed into `~/lab/.venv` or PSI application → executes in the service's trust context → changes door/barrier logic or selectively suppresses SIEM output while ordinary samples continue. This is a supplier/update pathway, distinct from a local attacker merely editing a file.

**Confidence & Provenance:** **High for the earlier recorded source/package observations:** inspected Python imports include pymodbus and `urllib.request`; metadata reported pymodbus 3.15.0, Flask 3.1.3 and gunicorn 26.2.0 on November 17. This does not attest loaded versions or identify a vulnerability in them. **Medium for the engineering inference** that modified dependencies could selectively suppress events: the inherited runbook describes historical W4 Attack B, not independently reproduced here. **Low confidence in applicability of the complete supplier-compromise chain:** publisher/build access, delivery mechanism, artifact verification and current attacker reach are unverified. **Unknown** whether packages are malicious, hash-pinned or approved. Hash baselines identify observed files, not trusted origin. Armatura's legitimate vulnerable embedded component is an analogy, not evidence of malicious packages or compromised publishers in PSI.

**Physical impact:** altered command logic could release a protected door, move a barrier unexpectedly, or obstruct emergency access if actual actuators depended on that logic. Selective event suppression could hide those actions and delay intervention. Real actuator connectivity and fail-safe behavior were not verified; the lab exposes simulated state only.

**Advisory integration:** [ICSA-26-274-01](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/ics-advisories/icsa-26-274-01) shows how a supplied access-control platform can inherit code-execution exposure from embedded ActiveMQ (**CVE-2023-46604**). That is a vulnerable legitimate dependency, not evidence of a malicious upstream release. For PSI, the actionable lesson is to inventory transitive dependencies and verify delivered artifacts, not to declare pymodbus/Flask affected or install an Armatura patch.

**Detection opportunities — proposed:** independently administered release-manifest/SBOM comparison for application, package and unit changes; alert on the first unapproved executable change. Correlate independently captured command transactions with received SIEM events to detect selective omissions. Add external receipt-health checks for complete export loss. Neither normal samples nor a second reader of PSI's own values detects all coherent falsification. No trusted manifest, independent transaction collector or physical observation source was verified installed.

**Compensating controls — proposed:** when the software dependency cannot be removed, use tested hash-pinned builds, verified supplier signatures where available, a separate release identity, restricted runtime write/install permissions and known-good rollback artifacts outside `psi` control. Restrict the service's command/network privileges and authorize independent protective observation if technical assurance is unavailable. A validated signature supports artifact origin/integrity relative to a trusted signing key, not harmlessness or an uncompromised signer; functional acceptance still requires an approved test. PSI owner and host administrator are proposed implementation/acceptance roles. Effectiveness remains unknown until tested.

### Vector 2 — Compromised maintenance contractor or support integration

**Chain:** attacker compromises a supplier's support endpoint/account/token-handling process → uses trusted maintenance connectivity or a still-valid local identity → authenticated but unauthorized protective changes, potentially with surveillance inhibition. The supplier element is the external maintainer/support trust relationship; ordinary employee misuse alone would not be a supply-chain compromise.

**Confidence & Provenance:** **High that the received scenario summaries report** vendor identity `svc_maint_vendor04` surviving corporate disablement and an unexpired response-connector exception (handoff sections 2/4/7; update basis/scope). This is documentary scenario evidence, not a newly verified host exposure. **Low for current lab applicability of the complete supplier-support pathway:** vendor relationship, actual remote route, cached identity interface, permissions and present connectivity are unverified. **Unknown** whether a supplier was compromised, who used the credential or whether it remains usable. Inspected HMI source contains shared authentication followed by coil-write logic; no successful login/write test or token retrieval/replay occurred. That source does not establish a supplier account or compromise.

**Physical impact:** unauthorized maintenance actions could defeat access protection and conceal entry, enabling material theft or equipment tampering; malicious barrier/lock actions could instead injure personnel or delay emergency access. None of those outcomes was established. The scenario's confirmed protective impairment must remain distinct from unresolved passage/material consequences and from unverified lab equivalence.

**Advisory integration:** Armatura **CVE-2026-94591/-94592** illustrate supplier-designed reusable secret exposure; **CVE-2026-94593/-94594** show how host logs, backups and support bundles can disclose credentials. These findings justify reviewing supplier support-artifact handling and per-installation secrets. They do not prove a compromised contractor, cached PSI token exposure or the same credential storage in this lab. The advisory's OS/configuration/log access prerequisites matter; these are not all unauthenticated remote-execution flaws.

**Detection opportunities — proposed:** correlate a named supplier session with independently approved asset/action/window, not just `auth.success`; alert on the first unapproved protective change and review unknown authorization immediately. Audit off-window support sessions, expired connector exceptions, local revocation acknowledgments and active-session invalidation. Use disposable authorized test identities for negative revocation checks, with the received five-minute target explicitly treated as a proposed design choice. Supplier session records, approval integration, the scenario cache interface and independent audit sources remain unverified.

**Compensating controls — proposed:** if an actual supplier-support dependency is identified, require named least-privilege, time-bounded access through an approved MFA gateway, supervised maintenance and independent approval/expiry for protective overrides. Separate telemetry from command-capable response access; restrict direct protocol access through validated enforcement rather than assuming HMI authentication covers Modbus. Inventory and revoke local/cached credentials and active sessions as well as directory identities. Limit support-bundle access, remove unnecessary secrets from newly prepared exports without destroying held evidence, and rotate confirmed exposed credentials through an approved dependency-aware plan. IAM/network/security roles should verify denied reuse and preserved emergency access; no such change or test was performed here and effectiveness remains unknown.

### Vector 3 — Vulnerable supplier access-controller/camera stack or replacement firmware

**Chain:** an integrator delivers an access-control stack with vulnerable embedded middleware/default secrets, or an attacker substitutes a vendor firmware/replacement-device artifact → deployed component exposes privileged interfaces or falsifies access/video evidence → unauthorized passage or unsafe barrier/egress behavior. Vulnerable legitimate software and deliberately tampered firmware are different subcases; only the former is illustrated by the retrieved advisory.

**Confidence & Provenance:** **High source confidence** for CISA's Armatura/ActiveMQ findings, affected ranges and credential prerequisites. **Low for direct applicability to PSI:** the runbook's real-world protocol examples do not establish physical devices, Armatura/ActiveMQ, installed versions, connectivity or firmware inventory. Product presence and exploitability are **unknown**, not confirmed exposure. Firmware substitution/supplier compromise are **unsupported hypotheses**, not conclusions from this advisory or the host checks. Obtain inventory before selecting a product-specific fix; these checks were not a comprehensive supplier-compromise investigation.

**Physical impact:** a compromised real access-controller/camera stack could permit unauthorized entry while falsifying observation, move a barrier unsafely, or leave an emergency route obstructed. These depend on actual device integration, mechanical safeguards and failure behavior. A reported normal coil or camera-alert register cannot establish that the physical counterpart works or has recovered.

**Advisory integration:** this is the closest product-class match to Armatura One, but still **not a confirmed product match**. The advisory identifies host execution/access-control takeover potential in Armatura One **<4.7.2** and USA **<4.6.1**; supported fixes are **V4.7.2 / V4.6.1_USA**. If inventory actually identifies an affected deployment, obtain its correct release from official support and assess operational impact before installation. If no match exists, retain the component/secret-design lessons without claiming those CVEs affect PSI. Malicious replacement firmware is a separate hypothetical subcase, not a finding in this advisory.

**Detection opportunities — proposed:** first obtain supplier/model/version, firmware provenance, embedded-component/SBOM and maintenance records; compare exact versions to the advisory. Monitor unapproved firmware/configuration changes and unexpected management exposure using a trusted baseline. Compare reported access/video state against separately trusted contacts, position or qualified observation if available; none was verified here. Prefer inventory/vendor evidence over broad active scanning of operational equipment. A verified affected product/version match establishes an affected asset; assess connectivity and each CVE's access prerequisites separately before asserting a reachable exploit path. Neither proves exploitation or supplier compromise. Detection effectiveness remains unknown.

**Compensating controls — proposed:** where replacement/patching must wait, engineering and operations should approve narrowly restricted management access, isolation that preserves required monitoring/emergency access, and guard/observer coverage where needed. Require trusted artifact verification and vendor lifecycle/support commitments for replacement acceptance; eliminate defaults and rotate exposed secrets through approved procedures. Only a verified affected Armatura deployment warrants its product-specific fix. Close remediation with witnessed access/egress and rollback checks, not an upgrade receipt or normal dashboard alone. Physical-security engineering, supply-chain management and operations own this acceptance.

## Current CISA ICS advisory — live retrieval

**[ICSA-26-274-01 — Armatura LLC Armatura One](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/ics-advisories/icsa-26-274-01)**, released October 1, 2026, retrieved live November 17 UTC (November 16 MST) using web search and official-page `webfetch`. This is a current retrieved advisory, not necessarily the latest issued by CISA. The recorded physical-access search returned third-party discussions of Armatura; the targeted CISA ICS search for `pymodbus` returned no results. The official advisory, not those search summaries, is the source for the findings below.

**Retrieval for the follow-up request:** read the full official advisory on November 17, 2026 UTC (November 16 MST), with a clock check immediately after retrieval at **06:25:02 UTC**. The page shows initial publication **2026-10-01**, revision **1**, dated **2026-10-01**; no later revision is listed. | Retrieved finding | Exact applicability / interpretation |
|---|---|
| Armatura One **<4.7.2** and USA **<4.6.1** affected | Vendor fixes listed as 4.7.2 and 4.6.1_USA; obtain through official support and validate recovery/egress before deployment. No reason to install Armatura software on this Python lab. |
| **CVE-2023-46604**, embedded Apache ActiveMQ OpenWire deserialization before authentication; CVSS v3.1 **9.8** | Could yield highest-privilege host execution in affected product. Illustrates Vector 1/3 transitive component risk; it is not a pymodbus/Flask CVE and does not establish PSI exposure. |

More precisely, **CVE-2026-94592** assigns a fixed vendor-defined database-superuser password; exploitation requires server-OS access and that the password has not been changed. **CVE-2026-94593** logs the database-superuser password in backup/restore commands on the host. **CVE-2026-94594** logs broker client credentials during normal operation; anyone able to read the log or an included backup/support bundle may recover them. These are distinct access prerequisites, not five interchangeable remote-code-execution flaws.

**Official mitigations read in this retrieval:** upgrade the main release line to **V4.7.2** or the USA line to **V4.6.1_USA**, using official technical support for acquisition/application guidance. The page describes main-line upgrades from V4.7.1 or earlier and USA upgrades from V4.3.1_USA or earlier, while its affected-version list is broader: all USA versions below 4.6.1. Confirm the supported upgrade path with the vendor rather than interpreting that wording as an unaffected intermediate range. CISA also recommends minimizing internet exposure, firewalling and isolation from business networks, maintained VPN-based remote access where required, and impact/risk assessment before defensive changes. These recommendations do not establish that PSI already has those controls. Credential rotation, independent collection and safe-function acceptance in this assessment are proposed adaptations, not claims of actions completed or verbatim vendor instructions.

**Applicability decision: related-risk illustration, not a confirmed direct match.** The observed system is a Python/Modbus simulation; earlier package metadata identified pymodbus, Flask and gunicorn. No Armatura product, embedded ActiveMQ instance, affected firmware or support bundle was established. A targeted CISA ICS search for `pymodbus` returned no results; that is a search limitation, not proof that the package has no vulnerabilities. This advisory illustrates vulnerable supplied middleware, reusable supplier secrets and support-artifact exposure in physical-access systems. It does not show that PSI contains these CVEs, that a supplier was maliciously compromised, or that a patch should be installed on the lab. First obtain product/version/SBOM and supplier-support inventory; apply a product-specific fix only after a verified match and approved change plan.

CISA states it is unaware of exploitation specifically targeting Armatura One for these findings; CVE-2023-46604 is in KEV and has been used against other ActiveMQ deployments. Therefore neither “no exploitation anywhere” nor “Armatura/PSI is actively compromised” follows. Advisory facts have **high source confidence**; actual lab product/version match remains **unestablished**.

CISA recommends minimized network exposure and isolation from business networks. For PSI, loopback binding alone does not demonstrate isolation between shared-host accounts; cross-account reachability and additional enforcement were not tested. Validated host/network enforcement and separate service trust domains are the relevant adaptation, not a claim that a drawn Purdue boundary already blocks access.
